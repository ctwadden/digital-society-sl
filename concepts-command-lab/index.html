The command changes the answer.

A concept tells you what to notice. A command term tells you what thinking to show. Try the matching activity before using the definitions to check.

Command decoder: the Pulse case

Fictional practice scenario: Pulse is a music app. It records songs played and skipped. Its algorithm ranks recommendations using those records, ranking repeatedly skipped types lower. Musicians reach listeners through these recommendations. These are not research findings about real apps.

Match each response to a command term. Use each command once.

Class 2 repair clinic: Improve “Pulse helps everyone.” First explain one specific effect using how/why. Then evaluate it by adding a limitation and a reasoned judgement. Do not invent evidence. These brief practice models demonstrate kinds of thinking, not complete examination answers.

Case A / Who gets to participate online?
Q5 focus: Evaluate Australia’s decision to require age-restricted social-media platforms to take reasonable steps to prevent under-16s holding accounts.

Content: networks and the internet; media; data. Contexts: social; political.

Age assurance means methods used to check or estimate a user’s age. This case concerns Australia, not a Canadian rule.

A1 / REGULATOR’S RESEARCH UPDATE

Early insights from eSafety’s comprehensive evaluation project ↗

eSafety Commissioner • 31 July 2026

Read: the opening findings, then the final paragraphs beginning “While the majority…” and “As the data…”. Skip the enforcement/legal detail.

Blocked link? Read the backup summary

eSafety’s July release reports data collected in March 2026, three months after Australia’s restrictions began. Reported under-16 account holding fell from 52.4% to 42.1%; platform use, including without accounts, fell from 85.9% to 81.5%. Some children reported connection difficulties; others reported benefits. eSafety says it is too early to judge whether the policy’s intended outcomes have been achieved.

Original paraphrase, not the full article or a direct quotation. Cite [A1 summary].

Watch the evidence: Early findings are not a final verdict. The account change is 10.3 percentage points, not 10.3%. This is not a legal finding of platform non-compliance.

A2 / RESEARCH-INFORMED OPINION

Restrictions Alone Won’t Keep Kids Safe Online. Here’s What Research Says Might ↗

UNICEF USA • Michael J. Nyenhuis • 4 September 2026

Read: only the section headed “A tolerant, supportive parenting style helps build judgment”. Stop at the next heading.

Blocked link? Read the backup summary

Nyenhuis argues that restrictions should be accompanied by supportive relationships and digital literacy. He draws on research to support conversations in which young people question what they encounter online. Rules can play a role, but the article argues they are insufficient on their own. This is an opinion article, not an evaluation of Australia’s policy.

Original paraphrase, not the full article or a direct quotation. Cite [A2 summary].

Watch the evidence: Use this as a perspective on complementary approaches. Do not claim it proves that Australia’s restrictions have failed.

Does a fall in account ownership prove that young people are safer?

Whose opportunities to connect or express themselves might change?

What can an early study establish—and what remains unknown?

Case B / Does AI help us learn—or just finish?
Q5 focus: Evaluate the use of AI assistants such as Tutor CoPilot to support human tutors in school mathematics.

Content: artificial intelligence; algorithms and code; data. Contexts: human knowledge; social.

Tutor CoPilot suggests responses to a human tutor. The tutor can choose or edit them. In this case, AI supports a human; it does not replace the tutor.

B1 / RESEARCH NEWS SUMMARY

Research Notes: Two Emerging Strategies for Using AI in Tutoring ↗

Stanford NSSA / FutureEd • 17 February 2026

Read: the introduction and the Tutor CoPilot section beginning “A second study…”. You do not need the separate LearnLM example.

Blocked link? Read the backup summary

This research summary describes Tutor CoPilot, which suggests responses that human tutors can choose or edit. In the reported study, students supported by tutors using it were four percentage points more likely to master mathematics topics. The largest gains occurred with lower-rated tutors. The result concerns a particular tutoring approach, not all AI tools or the removal of human teachers.

Original paraphrase, not the full article or a direct quotation. Cite [B1 summary].

Watch the evidence: This is a 2026 summary of earlier research, not a study conducted in February 2026. Topic mastery is not proof of lasting achievement in every classroom.

B2 / RESEARCH SYNTHESIS

OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026 ↗

OECD • 19 January 2026

Read: go to “Key messages”. Read the messages about task performance versus learning, pedagogical intent, and augmenting human teaching and tutoring.

Blocked link? Read the backup summary

The OECD distinguishes completing a task successfully with AI from learning to do it independently. Its report argues that educational purpose and design matter: some uses can support learning and human teaching, whereas outsourcing thinking to a general-purpose tool may not. It calls attention to preserving teachers’ agency rather than treating all AI use as equivalent.

Original paraphrase, not the full report or a direct quotation. Cite [B2 summary].

Watch the evidence: A broad synthesis helps judge conditions and risks; it does not directly measure Tutor CoPilot in your school.

Who decides what feedback the learner receives?

Is success on today’s task the same as independent learning tomorrow?

Would one result justify using every AI tool with every learner?

Case C / The cloud has a physical footprint
Q5 focus: Evaluate continued expansion of data centres in Ireland, considering benefits to digital-service users and implications for local communities.

Content: computing devices; networks and the internet; artificial intelligence. Contexts: environmental; economic.

A data centre houses computers that store and process data. “The cloud” still uses physical infrastructure. TWh means terawatt-hours, a unit of electrical energy.

C1 / OFFICIAL STATISTICS + INFOGRAPHIC

Data Centres Metered Electricity Consumption 2025 ↗

Central Statistics Office, Ireland • 7 July 2026

Read: study the infographic and its text transcript. Record the year, location, unit and what is being measured.

Blocked link? Read the backup summary

Ireland’s Central Statistics Office reports that data centres accounted for 23% of the country’s metered electricity consumption in 2025, compared with 22% in 2024 and 5% in 2015. The infographic also reports 18% for urban dwellings and 9% for rural dwellings in 2025. These figures cover all data centres, not AI alone.

Original paraphrase, not the full release or a direct quotation. Cite [C1 summary].

Watch the evidence: The release is from 2026, but the data are from 2025. Ireland is not the whole world; electricity is not all energy. Urban plus rural dwellings total 27%, not 18%.

C2 / ENERGY ANALYSIS + PROJECTIONS

Key Questions on Energy and AI — Executive summary ↗

International Energy Agency • 16 April 2026

Read: the updated electricity-demand outlook and the section on whether data centres raise electricity prices. Find the paragraph beginning “AI has the potential…” for possible energy-system benefits.

Blocked link? Read the backup summary

The IEA projects global data-centre electricity demand rising from about 485 TWh in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030. It examines grid and supply pressures, while explaining that price effects depend on local conditions. AI could also help operate energy systems more efficiently. The future figures are projections, not measurements, and the global analysis is not specific to Ireland.

Original paraphrase, not the full report or a direct quotation. Cite [C2 summary].

Watch the evidence: Do not label all data-centre demand as AI demand. Potential grid benefits are not guaranteed, and a global projection does not predict a particular Irish community’s outcome.

Who benefits from a global digital service, and who hosts its infrastructure?

What does the infographic measure: all energy, electricity, or AI alone?

Which claims describe measured results, and which describe possible futures?

Your individual assessment

Five responses. Twenty marks.

Stay with the same case. You may reuse relevant evidence, but each answer must do the different thinking requested. You are not graded on graphic design or a slideshow.

Q1/2

Identify two stakeholders in your chosen case.

A stakeholder is a person, group or organisation that affects a decision or is affected by it. Give two precise names or groups. Avoid “everyone”.

1 mark for each relevant, distinct stakeholder.
Q2/4

Explain how two concepts help you understand your case.

About 80–100 words total. For each: “By [concept], I mean… In this case, [specific detail] reveals…”. Go beyond attaching a label.

For each concept: accurate meaning (1) + explained, specific case connection (1).
Q3/4

Explain one impact on one stakeholder.

About 60–80 words. Which feature of the digital system—or decision about its regulation—produces the effect? How? Why does it matter to this stakeholder? Add a source detail.

Accurate impact (1), mechanism (1), causal link to stakeholder (1), supporting source detail (1).
Q4/4

Analyse how one feature or decision connects impacts on two stakeholders.

About 90–110 words. Trace: feature/decision → impact on A → relationship with B. Show a trade-off, dependency or feedback—not two unrelated lists.

Feature/decision with source support (1), impact on A (1), impact on B (1), relationship between them (1).
Q5/6

Evaluate your case’s focus question.

Use the exact Q5 focus question printed in your Case A, B or C reading pack.

About 140–180 words. Use BOTH sources. Develop a strength and a limitation. Weigh them using a criterion such as safety, learning, fairness or sustainability. End with a justified, possibly conditional judgement.

Evidence-linked strength (0–2), evidence-linked limitation (0–2), weighing and justified judgement (0–2). For strength/limitation: 0 = missing or inaccurate; 1 = relevant but asserted or weakly evidenced; 2 = developed with source support. For judgement: 0 = missing/indefensible; 1 = limited weighing; 2 = a clear criterion, weighing and a justified conclusion with a condition or uncertainty. With only one source, strength and limitation are each capped at 1/2; judgement is assessed normally.

This /20 score is teacher-created classroom practice, not an official IB examination mark scheme or a conversion to an IB 1–7 grade. There are no separate marks for length, appearance, speed or partner participation.
