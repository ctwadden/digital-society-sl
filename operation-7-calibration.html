Practice question: Evaluate the extent to which the British Library cyberattack shows that power in digital society depends on control of infrastructure.

These are teacher-created practice responses, not official IB materials. Diagnose the missing or successful gates before reading the commentary.

Response A · Descriptive and incomplete

The British Library was attacked by hackers and many services stopped working. This shows that technology is powerful because people depend on computers. The attack was bad for staff and researchers. Better cybersecurity could have prevented the problem. Overall, infrastructure is very important in digital society.

Diagnosis: Names the event but does not explain the mechanism, attribute evidence, differentiate stakeholders, use the 3Cs analytically or qualify the conclusion. Gates 2–7 are missing or superficial.

Response B · Secure

To a considerable extent, the British Library case shows that control of infrastructure creates power because privileged access and tightly connected services can shape whether other people can use knowledge systems. The Library reported that attackers removed about 600 GB of data and damaged server infrastructure, while the physical collections remained safe. This distinction matters for researchers and staff: collection integrity survived, but the systems for discovering and requesting material were unavailable. The concept of systems therefore reveals how dependence on connected infrastructure converted a technical breach into institutional disruption. However, infrastructure was not the only source of power because staff maintained some functions and recovery decisions also involved expertise, investment and public responsibility. The case therefore supports the claim strongly in the short term, although the extent depends on which service and stakeholder is considered.

Diagnosis: Clear technology/mechanism, attributed evidence, stakeholder difference, a useful concept, impact, counter-perspective and conditional extent. Source limitation and explicit trade-off could be stronger.

Response C · Grade-7-oriented thinking

To a large but qualified extent, the British Library cyberattack demonstrates that power in digital society depends on control of infrastructure, because privileged access and system architecture determined who could interrupt—or restore—the institution’s public knowledge function. The Library’s incident review reports both large-scale data removal and damage to server infrastructure; the ICO later connected escalation of the incident to the absence of multi-factor authentication on an administrator account. Used together, these sources support a cautious mechanism from privileged access to wider service disruption, although neither public account establishes every attacker action. The unequal effects are central: the physical collections remained safe, yet researchers lost discovery and request capabilities while staff moved to constrained recovery processes. Systems reveals interdependence, while power identifies who controlled access, safeguards and restoration priorities. A longer-term implication may be reduced trust or unequal knowledge access if recovery remains partial, but this is conditional rather than inevitable. A counter-perspective is that institutional expertise, transparent reporting and staged recovery redistributed some power back to staff and the public. The trade-off is that rapid openness may conflict with secure, validated restoration. Infrastructure control therefore shaped power most strongly during disruption, but its longer-term extent depended on governance, expertise, recovery investment and the institution’s commitment to access.

Diagnosis: All seven gates interact. Evidence is attributed and limited; the mechanism drives stakeholder analysis; concepts change the explanation; implication, counter-perspective and trade-off alter the final degree judgement.
