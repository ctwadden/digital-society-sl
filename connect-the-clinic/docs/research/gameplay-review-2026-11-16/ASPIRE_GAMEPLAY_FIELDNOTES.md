# Aspire gameplay field notes: the contract is the unit of play

**Research date:** 16 November 2026, America/Halifax. **Status:** complete for this documentary research assignment. **Owner:** gameplay research specialist; integration and current-build conclusions belong to the lead.

## Main finding

The useful reference is a professional role with a sequence of consequential decisions. A cable operation can be an excellent controls exercise, but it is too small to carry the promise of a client-consulting game. Our design question should be: **what responsibility does the player take, what can they decide, and what changes because of that decision?**

The evidence below supports that reading of Aspire. The recommendations are original design proposals for the clinic, not claims about unobserved Aspire mechanics. No installer was downloaded, no game executable was operated, and no video was watched in this research pass. I read original research, a contemporary firsthand walkthrough, Cisco material and clearly identified historical reproductions. The separate visual specialist owns direct screenshot inspection.

## 2. What a player actually undertakes

### CCNA Edition: a staged entry into professional work

The CCNA walkthrough moves from a phone call and office visit to an internship, a multi-task contract, PC configuration and a scored handover. It presents the game as practice alongside teaching. Historical retail unlock tokens do not establish a fictional charge for using help. [E3]

**Interpretation:** the introduction establishes a job role and a route into work. The important transition is from listening to a person into taking responsibility for the requested result. A branded map or longer conversation would not reproduce that responsibility by itself.

### Older studied build: the first two contracts already combine choices

A café job requires four PCs within a budget: four new units require borrowing, while refurbished ones provide another route. A referral leads to an employment-agency task combining PCs, interview accessories, three wired workstations, one wireless workstation and DHCP. The study also documents tutorials, reference help and retained equipment. These descriptions concern its older studied build. [E2]

### A later technical challenge: diagnose a partially working office

The creator study's medical-office contract starts with one working PC and three failing ones. Players compare configurations, repair and retest. Manual repairs or DHCP on faulty clients can work; changing the reference PC violates that contract's acceptance condition. It is not a universal warning against DHCP. The study also reports beginner difficulties with controls, cautioning against confusing interface difficulty with networking understanding. [E1]

**Interpretation:** the interesting task includes uncertainty and something worth preserving. “Inspect before changing” becomes useful because indiscriminate changes can damage a previously satisfactory condition. A permanently highlighted faulty port removes most of that diagnostic decision.

## 4. Ten transferable design findings

These are **our design judgments**, informed by the evidence but not claims that Aspire implemented the proposed clinic behavior.

### 1. Make the contract the visible unit of progress

“Get the clinic ready to open” can contain several supported operations without showing all the tools at once. Put a concise outcome-oriented brief above the first taught action, so the student understands that plugging in a cable is one contribution to a larger job. The public title of the mission should match the scale of responsibility it actually offers.

**Acceptance:** after the opening, a student can explain who needs help and what must work at handover, without naming interface buttons.

### 2. Keep a small controls rehearsal, then release responsibility

The previous clinic design report deliberately selected a trainer-prepared missing-link task. That is a useful onboarding decision, but it should be presented as a brief rehearsal. A suggested 60–120-second target is a design hypothesis to pilot, not a measured duration. Continue to teach unfamiliar addressing concepts before expecting independent application; do not make the whole mission a sequence of revealed answers.

**Acceptance:** the player soon encounters a decision for which the next button does not disclose the correct solution.

### 3. Begin client work with information to discover

A receptionist could report that the appointment service is unavailable, while a staff workstation still works. The player decides whom to ask, what to inspect and what test to run first. Conversation should reveal requirements or symptoms that change the plan. Avoid adding dialogue that merely restates the task card.

**Acceptance:** useful information is obtained through at least one inspection or question, and the player can state how it affected their decision.

### 4. Make equipment selection an actual trade-off

Offer a small set of feasible, distinct choices: reuse a suitable available computer, repair a supported component, or buy a replacement with a different cost or capability. Avoid a shop where every item is required, one is plainly best, or buying everything is harmless. Let the brief establish the relevant capability; decorative specifications should not create false realism.

**Acceptance:** at least two affordable solutions meet the brief and have an explainable consequence. The budget is never a trap that permanently prevents a student finishing.

### 5. Distinguish operations that solve different causes

Physical connection, power, address configuration, service availability and authorization should have different evidence and remedies. The player should learn when a tool is relevant. After taught IP practice, a physical fault should resist an address-only “repair” because the model still lacks a physical path.

**Acceptance:** a plausible but irrelevant change does not produce success, and the next observation supports a better diagnosis.

### 6. Give the player a correct part of the system to protect

A partially working clinic makes restraint meaningful. A good solution fixes the failed service without disrupting an existing one. Preserve/undo and before/after tests allow experimentation without reducing the task to a forced restart. Contract acceptance must distinguish a specific client requirement from a universal technical law.

**Acceptance:** a repair that improves one station while breaking another cannot complete the contract; the feedback identifies the affected service.

### 7. Let the place explain the work

Room changes should correspond to different people, equipment or permissions: reception handles bookings, a staff workstation needs a different information scope, the equipment area holds shared infrastructure, and an off-site user sits outside the clinic boundary. Clicking a room can be sufficient navigation. Movement should reveal context or enable work, not consume time between identical panels.

**Acceptance:** changing location changes at least one relevant observation, action or constraint.

### 8. Make progress visible in people’s work

A current technical pass should enable a model-backed service demonstration: a synthetic booking lookup succeeds, a staff status changes, or an allowed request passes while a restricted request fails. Do not replace this with a celebratory badge that appears after a button sequence. Human feedback should match the actual configuration and access policy.

**Acceptance:** the same service visibly fails before repair and works afterward; privacy tests cover both an authorized and an unauthorized request.

### 9. Use money and relationships as consequences, not academic proxies

A closing ledger should show the player's actual purchases, accepted guidance costs and earnings. A follow-up request can make the client relationship feel persistent. Preserve free practice, explain consultation costs before acceptance and cap them. Money, speed, hints and completed customers must not automatically produce IB grades or mastery claims.

**Acceptance:** the teacher summary records decisions, observations and the student's reasoning separately from the game economy.

### 10. Escalate the type of responsibility before increasing map size

An original progression could move from restoring one service, to equipping a small workspace, to diagnosing mixed faults, to separating access, then to a later inter-network job. Each step should change what the player has to reason about. Routers, DHCP, subnetting or remote services need causal model support before they appear as assessed tasks. New client names and scenery alone are not new levels.

**Acceptance:** every added mission specifies one new responsibility, one prerequisite, a plausible wrong approach, a recoverable consequence and more than one reasonable path where the brief permits.

## 5. Implication for the next clinic revision

Keep the introduction the teacher likes. Keep the reliable authoritative model, export and recovery infrastructure. Reframe the short cabling activity as an explicitly bounded training moment, then make the first client episode prove the consulting premise: inspect a need, choose a plan, act on equipment/settings, test the service, handle a meaningful change, decide access and explain the outcome.

The lead should select one such complete episode before commissioning a city, avatars or more scenery. A useful design review asks: **what did the student decide that we did not decide for them, and what consequence could they see?** If the answer remains only “which free port”, the mission still falls short even with better graphics.
