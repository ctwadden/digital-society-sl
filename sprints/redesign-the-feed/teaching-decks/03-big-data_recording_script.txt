SLIDE 01 — Data records part of a situation  (save your recording as audio/03-big-data/slide-01.mp3)
A viewer might watch a video because they enjoy it, need it for a class or disagree with it. The recorded action is not identical to the viewer’s reason. Data analytics uses observations to find patterns or make predictions. We begin by distinguishing a recorded value from an inference. This matters when a platform decides what to show next. Our examples use invented records. We will not collect personal histories or require students to enter accounts to explore the mechanism.

SLIDE 02 — Big data and data analytics  (save your recording as audio/03-big-data/slide-02.mp3)
The textbook characterizes big data using four Vs. Data can be structured in a table or arrive in formats such as video, text and images. Analytics can describe what happened, detect patterns or support a prediction. A classroom table is small data, but it helps us reason about a process that operates on a much larger collection. A numerical output is not automatically a reliable answer. Ask how the records were collected, what they represent and how the analysis matches the question.

SLIDE 03 — Volume and velocity  (save your recording as audio/03-big-data/slide-03.mp3)
Consider a match where sensors generate records throughout play. Volume concerns how many records accumulate. Velocity concerns the rate at which new records arrive and whether analysis can keep up. A system that stores a large archive may have high volume without fast current arrival. A live stream may require quick decisions before every record can be manually checked. Explain which characteristic matters to a particular task instead of treating big data as a single vague advantage.

SLIDE 04 — Variety and veracity  (save your recording as audio/03-big-data/slide-04.mp3)
A platform may combine a video, its caption, a device setting and an interaction log. These are different kinds of data. That variety creates opportunities, but joining formats accurately can be difficult. Veracity concerns whether the values are trustworthy for the intended use. Repeated records, missing values, outdated labels and false accounts can all distort an analysis. Having many formats does not establish good quality. A large dataset can remain unrepresentative even when its individual records are accurate.

SLIDE 05 — Small and clean, or large and messy?  (save your recording as audio/03-big-data/slide-05.mp3)
These invented datasets force us to name a purpose. Dataset A may be suitable for checking one class’s activity, but it may not represent the whole school. Dataset B might capture a wider range of people, yet its errors require investigation. We cannot decide which is better without knowing the question, sampling, missingness and quality controls. Data cleaning can improve validity while accidentally removing groups if missing values are unevenly distributed. Make the decision and its assumption visible.

SLIDE 06 — A missing value is an unresolved question  (save your recording as audio/03-big-data/slide-06.mp3)
Suppose an item has no completion value. Replacing it with zero can push the creator down the ranking even though zero completion was never observed. Removing the item avoids the calculation but also removes its opportunity for exposure. Estimating a value may help, yet it relies on an assumption that needs checking. A sensible choice depends on the task and consequence. Record what you chose and how the result might change under another plausible choice.

SLIDE 07 — An association does not establish a cause  (save your recording as audio/03-big-data/slide-07.mp3)
Items with more exposure often receive more interactions. This could reflect relevance, the number of opportunities to be seen or both. Looking only at interaction totals cannot distinguish those explanations. A carefully designed comparison might hold exposure constant or compare a changed allocation, but even then we need to examine other factors and limits. The important habit is to state the measured relationship first, then ask which alternative explanations the evidence has ruled out.

SLIDE 08 — The data lifecycle creates decisions at every stage  (save your recording as audio/03-big-data/slide-08.mp3)
The chapter places big data inside a lifecycle. At collection, we decide what to record and whom to include. Storage involves access, security and organization. Use involves analysis and sharing for a purpose. Preservation and destruction concern how long records remain and what happens when they are no longer needed. This week’s assigned reading begins the collection dilemmas in section 3.1I. It does not teach all later lifecycle dilemmas. A diagram of a lifecycle is a map, not proof that every stage has been adequately managed.

SLIDE 09 — Collection can shape the eventual result  (save your recording as audio/03-big-data/slide-09.mp3)
The assigned page raises ethical collection, consent and excessive collection. We can ask what information is actually needed to answer a question, and whether a group is missing before analysis begins. Collecting an additional personal detail can create a privacy cost without improving the decision. Applicable rules depend on the setting and jurisdiction, so this is an ethical design discussion rather than a universal legal checklist. Explain the purpose, the proposed data and the effect of collecting or omitting it.

SLIDE 10 — Analytics can support several purposes  (save your recording as audio/03-big-data/slide-10.mp3)
The textbook gives historical examples from finance and sports. The enduring idea is the use of data to inform a decision. Describing last month’s activity, flagging an unusual transaction and predicting interest are different tasks. A prediction expresses an estimated outcome and should be evaluated against later observations. The examples do not make analytics infallible. A false alert can inconvenience someone, while a missed alert can leave a problem undetected. Identify the task and the cost of each kind of error.

SLIDE 11 — A bounded big-data inquiry  (save your recording as audio/03-big-data/slide-11.mp3)
A manageable inquiry identifies a particular use rather than asking whether all big data is good. For a platform, we could investigate whether recorded interactions represent new creators as well as established ones. The real case file gives a provider account and a regulator account with different purposes. Neither offers a complete dataset of individual experiences. Use the source passport to record the locator, claim, evidence and limitation. A limitation helps you decide what a source is useful for rather than simply dismissing it as biased.
