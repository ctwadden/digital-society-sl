# Algorithmic dilemmas: bias, judgement and accountability

3.2E · pp. 86–89; reflection p. 91

## 1. A correct calculation can produce a harmful decision

A ranking can be calculated exactly as designed and still cause problems. A system optimized for viewing time may give a viewer content they keep watching without serving their long-term interests. A hiring system may repeat a consistent rule that disadvantages applicants whose circumstances differ. We will distinguish mechanical correctness from justified decisions. The aim is to make a judgement supported by the mechanism, evidence and a clear criterion. The real TikTok case illustrates the tension, but our examples remain bounded by what the sources actually establish.

## 2. Replacing and supporting human judgement

Algorithms can reduce repetitive work and apply the same procedure across many cases. Consistency is valuable, but consistently making an unsuitable decision remains a problem. Human judgement can supply context absent from the input, yet human reviewers can also be biased. A meaningful review process requires access to the relevant information and power to overturn an output. Placing a person beside a system is insufficient if they simply accept every score. Ask who makes the final decision and what evidence they use.

## 3. Bias can enter at several points

Algorithmic bias concerns systematic skew or unfairness in decisions. The textbook identifies biased or incomplete data and choices made by developers. We can examine those choices more precisely. A location variable may stand in for access to resources rather than the ability we want to measure. A target based on previous approvals can repeat past exclusion. A threshold can create different error rates across groups. More data does not automatically solve these problems if it preserves the same omissions or labels.

## 4. Missing examples can change who succeeds

These numbers are invented to explain representation. They are not a finding about any company. Suppose a model is tested mostly on Group A. A high overall accuracy can coexist with poor performance for Group B because B contributes fewer test cases. Adding more examples from A increases the size without correcting B’s representation. A fair evaluation needs relevant data and disaggregated results, while taking care with small samples and privacy. Representation matters, but it is only one source of bias.

## 5. A proxy can misrepresent the intended quality

Imagine a school choosing volunteers for a media event. It ranks students using the number of previous events they completed. That count is a proxy for reliability. A student who was never offered an event cannot build the same count. The rule may confuse opportunity with willingness or ability. This is a synthetic scenario. To judge the rule, we would need information about access and performance, not just the final ranking. Explain the link between the proxy and the target rather than assuming a numerical score measures the desired quality directly.

## 6. A feedback loop can reinforce an early advantage

If the first ranking gives one creator more exposure, that creator has more opportunities to receive views and reactions. If the next ranking uses those totals, the original advantage can grow. This is a possible feedback mechanism, not proof of unfairness in every recommender. A creator might also receive exposure because people find the work useful. We need to examine the rule and compare appropriate measures. Explain each link and identify what could interrupt the loop, such as reserving opportunities for items with little previous exposure.

## 7. Human judgement can erode through over-reliance

A teacher, moderator or employer might accept a score without asking how it was produced. Over time, that habit can reduce the use of independent judgement. The issue is how people interact with the system, not simply whether software exists. Useful safeguards include recording a reason for a consequential decision, checking cases near a threshold and allowing a person to challenge incorrect input. These are proposals to evaluate, not guarantees. Each requires staff, time and information, and may create new privacy or workload costs.

## 8. A black box limits explanation

A black box algorithm produces an output whose internal reasoning is difficult to understand or explain. The textbook focuses on complex learned systems. A simple rule can also be opaque to the person affected if the operator refuses to disclose it. These are different barriers. Publishing a formula may help with secrecy, but a complex model may need other explanations and testing. A black box can still produce useful predictions. The question is whether the available evidence and explanation are adequate for the consequence of the decision.

## 9. Transparency makes parts of the decision understandable

A programmer, regulator and affected student need different levels of explanation. A technical model description may be useless to someone trying to correct their record. Transparency can disclose the objective, data source, decision procedure or reasons for a particular output. It can help people contest an error. It cannot by itself establish that the objective is fair or that the system causes no harm. Ask what has been disclosed, to whom, and what action that person can take with the information.

## 10. Accountability assigns responsibility and consequences

Accountability concerns responsibility for a decision and what happens when it goes wrong. A provider might explain a rule yet leave nobody responsible for correcting an unfair outcome. A review process needs a responsible decision-maker, a record of what happened and an effective route to remedy. Different organizations may share responsibility for data collection, model development and deployment. Our question is not simply who to blame. It is who can inspect, change or stop the process, and whether the affected person can obtain a meaningful response.

## 13. Observation, explanation and intention

Suppose three items receive more views than the others. That is an observation. A rule favouring previous engagement could explain how the pattern develops, but we need evidence about the rule and exposure. Claiming the operator intended to silence another creator adds a further inference. An uneven result does not establish that intention. A source describing a goal, an internal document or another suitable record may help investigate purpose. Keep these levels separate so an inquiry remains critical without exaggerating its evidence.

## 14. A conditional judgement weighs an intervention

A conditional judgement states what you recommend, why and under which conditions. For example, a discovery allocation could be justified if testing shows that it improves access for less-seen creators without exposing users to unacceptable content. That proposal could reduce immediate predicted engagement and requires eligibility checks. Transparency may help evaluate it, but outcome evidence is still needed. We have modelled the structure of reasoning on a different example. Your independent response must choose evidence and weigh its own conditions.
