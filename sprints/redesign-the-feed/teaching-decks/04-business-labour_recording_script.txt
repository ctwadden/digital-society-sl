# Digital business and labour: work, exchange and global connections

4.2A–D · pp. 213–227 · Arc A: 213–219; Arc B: 220–227

## 1. Digital systems change how organizations work

The economic context examines how people work and exchange goods and services. A business uses digital systems to coordinate employees, record transactions and understand demand. A platform can also decide which seller or creator a customer encounters. We will connect a system’s mechanism to opportunities and constraints for different people. A successful business outcome does not automatically mean every worker or customer benefits. This deck covers the assigned business and working-practice sections, and identifies the remainder of the economic chapter.

## 2. Operation and organization of a business

Consider a school event shop. Each sale updates a transaction record. Summing sales by item can inform the next stock order. A shared calendar helps volunteers coordinate, and a shared document records decisions. The system connects information to action. The textbook describes movement towards more dynamic teams, but that is a possible organizational change rather than a universal outcome of using software. A company can adopt digital tools while retaining a strict hierarchy. Explain the specific change in the example you use.

## 3. Customer value and business incentives

A subscription service may value renewal, a marketplace may earn a commission on completed sales, and an advertising-funded service may gain from opportunities to show ads. These are possible incentives, not proof of the objective used by every business. A platform’s ranking choice can support one of those outcomes. Distinguish a claim about the business model from evidence about an actual ranking rule. Consider customer value, operating costs and other constraints as well as revenue.

## 4. Transaction data becomes a decision

## 5. Diversification and workforce diversity

The chapter uses diversity in two related but different senses. A business may diversify what it sells, and it may seek a diverse workforce. These should not be confused. An inclusive organization also examines whether people can participate effectively, rather than simply counting demographic categories. Digital recruitment can broaden access, but an unsuitable screening rule can exclude applicants. The next two slides explain the textbook’s four diversification categories using invented examples, before we return to hiring and power.

## 10. Working practices and office design

The textbook describes start-ups, office design and digital working practices. A start-up is an organization in an early stage of development. Funding and product uncertainty can affect workers. Flexible spaces and shared tools can support coordination, yet open rooms can also distract people and some tasks need privacy. The historical descriptions of particular company offices are not current evidence of employee experience. Judge the arrangement against the task and the people who use it, including access, concentration and boundaries between work and personal time.

## 11. Remote work and digital nomadism

An employee working from home can be remote without being a digital nomad. A person travelling while completing online work may be both. The system requires suitable devices, networks, access to files and ways to coordinate. That can expand location choices, but time zones, unreliable access and data handling can introduce costs. Permission to work across borders depends on the specific setting, so a course discussion should investigate the relevant rules rather than assume freedom to work anywhere.

Source: Bomfim et al., Digital Society for the IB Diploma, Hodder, 2022. Printed pages cited per slide. Original paraphrase. Textbook connection: pp. 217–218.

## 12. Crowdsourcing and crowdfunding

A group asking people to submit designs is crowdsourcing. A group asking for donations to produce an exhibition is crowdfunding. One gathers contributions of work or information, and the other gathers money. A crowdsourced collection may contain errors or uneven perspectives, so someone still needs to evaluate it. Crowdfunding does not guarantee a project will succeed. Use a specific example and identify what is being contributed. No account creation, payment or participation in a labour platform is required for this lesson.

## 13. Microwork, gig work and sharing

These categories can overlap, but they emphasize different features. Labelling individual images for payment is microwork. A platform matching a customer with someone to complete a delivery illustrates gig work. Renting access to equipment through a booking service illustrates a sharing arrangement. A creator earning advertising revenue is not automatically performing microwork. Explain the task, payment arrangement and platform’s role before drawing comparisons. Avoid assuming the same working conditions across all digital work.

## 14. Platforms can mediate access to work

A matching platform reduces the effort needed to find a customer. It may also set the conditions under which a worker appears, receives a task or challenges a rating. That changes the power relationship. A numerical rating can affect visibility without revealing the context of a complaint. These are mechanisms to investigate in a specific case rather than universal findings about every platform. Trace the information that enters the allocation and identify who can change a record or contest the result.

## 15. Employee organizations and bargaining power

## 21. Monitoring can become a feedback loop

Suppose a fictional parcel centre records picking time. A manager compares a worker’s time with a target and sends a prompt to speed up. The next recorded time becomes feedback. A faster average does not establish safe work or explain why a particular worker took longer. Gamification adds game elements such as scores or badges to influence participation. It is not itself a measure of employee wellbeing. Explain both the processing rule and the worker’s ability to challenge an inaccurate record. This is a synthetic mechanism; it makes no claim about current Amazon practices.

Source: Hodder 2022, printed pp. 220–221. Original teaching paraphrase; synthetic examples unless a source is named.

## 22. E-commerce names the exchange, not the payment method

E-commerce means buying or selling goods or services online. Classify the parties to a particular transaction. A parts supplier selling stock to a repair shop is B2B. The repair shop selling a bracket to a customer is B2C. One person reselling a used bicycle to another is C2C, even if a business operates the marketplace connecting them. An online marketplace mediates listings, discovery or exchange; it need not be the seller of every item. A card payment is a means of settlement, not a fourth buyer/seller category. E-trading is the online exchange of financial products, a narrower activity within e-commerce.

Source: Hodder 2022, printed pp. 221–222. Original teaching paraphrase; synthetic examples unless a source is named.

## 24. Targeted marketing connects a signal to an audience

Microtargeting uses data to tailor content or advertisements to a selected audience. A fictional store might infer cycling interest from visits to helmet pages and display a bicycle advert. The visit is observed; the interest is inferred. A relevant advert may help a buyer discover a product, while collecting or reusing data can affect privacy. A click supports the claim that an advert was clicked, not that it persuaded the buyer or improved their life. To evaluate success, ask what was measured, what was omitted, and whether the comparison supports a causal claim. Do not ask students to disclose personal advertising histories.

Source: Hodder 2022, printed pp. 222. Original teaching paraphrase; synthetic examples unless a source is named.

## 25. Currency and payment are different layers

An online sale can be priced in Canadian dollars and paid using a card or bank transfer. That does not make the currency a cryptocurrency. Money can function as a medium of exchange, a unit of account and a store of value; the Bank of Canada’s February 2026 explanation provides a current Canadian context. Cryptocurrency systems use cryptographic methods and distributed-ledger arrangements; their governance and rights vary. Do not repeat the textbook’s broad statement that all cryptocurrency is unregulated as a universal current fact. This lesson classifies systems; it is not an investment activity. No purchase, wallet or account is needed.

Source: Hodder 2022, printed pp. 223. Original teaching paraphrase; synthetic examples unless a source is named. Current-context source checked 11 October 2026: https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2026/02/what-is-money/

## 26. A token record does not automatically transfer copyright

The textbook’s NFT discussion is a historical starting point. Distinguish an identifier recorded on a ledger from the associated image or music and from permission to reproduce that work. WIPO’s explanatory article warns that an NFT purchase does not generally transfer copyright; a separate rights agreement may do so. A token record alone is therefore not sufficient evidence of permission to print or reuse an associated picture. The value of a token and the authenticity of the associated work are also different questions. Use an invented digital art example, not a trading exercise.

## 28. Cashless convenience can create an access barrier

Consider a fictional school community market that accepts only an app payment. A connected customer may pay quickly; another customer without the required account cannot buy. An outage can interrupt both groups. A cashless society is the stronger idea of all transactions being electronic, not merely increasing card use. Compare convenience, access, privacy and resilience using an explicit stakeholder. A defensible response might preserve a cash or assisted alternative, but test its costs and reliability. Do not treat old textbook global banking or Swedish payment percentages as current data.

## 29. Small purchases can add up

Microtransactions are small purchases such as virtual items inside an app. A loot box offers a random or mystery reward; not all microtransactions do. In a synthetic example, twelve purchases at two dollars total twenty-four dollars. A low individual price is not a spending limit. Evaluate clearer cost display, purchase confirmation or limits against the player’s control and the developer’s business model. Rules vary by jurisdiction; this deck makes no blanket claim that every loot box is legally gambling or universally banned. No real purchases or gaming accounts are required.

## 30. From a digital model to a physical object

Additive manufacturing builds material up; subtractive manufacturing removes material from a larger piece. A simple filament printer commonly takes a CAD-derived model, uses slicing software to plan layers and paths, then deposits heated material. The conceptual workshop image shows a non-critical bottle holder; it does not certify a safe bicycle part. The operator still checks dimensions and suitability. Potential reductions in offcuts do not guarantee lower total environmental impact: material, energy, failed prints and the comparison method matter. No printer operation is assigned in this DS lesson. Explain the digital-to-physical mechanism and evaluate a conditional outcome.

## 32. Globalization connects customers, suppliers and workers

A fictional Halifax design shop sells a downloadable template to an overseas customer and buys a font licence from an overseas supplier. The first is borderless selling; the second is global sourcing. International reach can create opportunity for a small firm, but access, language, fulfilment and different rules can constrain it. For physical goods, shipping and returns still matter. An environmental or labour advantage needs evidence about actual practices, not simply the supplier’s location or a lower price. Explain how a networked ordering or collaboration system changes reach before making a judgement.

Source: Hodder 2022, printed pp. 225–226. Original teaching paraphrase; synthetic examples unless a source is named.

## 33. Location and organizational boundary are separate questions

The textbook’s page 226 wording combines offshoring and outsourcing. Teach a more precise analytical distinction: offshoring concerns geography; outsourcing concerns the organizational boundary. A company-owned overseas support team is offshore but still in-house. A local contractor is outsourced but not offshore. An overseas contractor can be both. Returning operations from abroad is reshoring; moving a task back inside is insourcing. The chapter also uses inshoring for a return from abroad and nearsourcing for operations closer to end markets. State the meaning you use rather than merging location with ownership.

Source: Hodder 2022, printed pp. 226. Original teaching paraphrase; synthetic examples unless a source is named.

## 34. A judgement compares whose costs and gains count

Bring the chapter together through power, systems and change. A cheaper remote supplier may lower a firm’s immediate cost but create a dependency or change local employment. A longer-distance supplier is not automatically worse; compare the actual service, transport, labour conditions and alternatives. State an explicit criterion, a mechanism, evidence and a limitation before recommending a policy. The chapter’s reflection on page 227 returns to these trade-offs. For a P1-style explanation, show the intermediate operation. For P2-style preparation, compare what two sources support and synthesize a conditional answer; do not treat a single-source paragraph as a full Paper 2.
