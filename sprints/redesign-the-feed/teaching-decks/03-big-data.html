A viewer might watch a video because they enjoy it, need it for a class or disagree with it. The recorded action is not identical to the viewer’s reason. The textbook characterizes big data using four Vs. Data can be structured in a table or arrive in formats such as video, text and images. Analytics can describe what happened, detect patterns or support a prediction. A classroom table is small data, but it helps us reason about a process that operates on a much larger collection. Consider a match where sensors generate records throughout play. Volume concerns how many records accumulate. Velocity concerns the rate at which new records arrive and whether analysis can keep up. A system that stores a large archive may have high volume without fast current arrival. A live stream may require quick decisions before every record can be manually checked. Explain which characteristic matters to a particular task instead of treating big data as a single vague advantage.

A million records contain the same incorrect duration unit. Which V is directly affected?

Read the explanation and source notes

A platform may combine a video, its caption, a device setting and an interaction log. These are different kinds of data. That variety creates opportunities, but joining formats accurately can be difficult. Veracity concerns whether the values are trustworthy for the intended use. Repeated records, missing values, outdated labels and false accounts can all distort an analysis. Having many formats does not establish good quality. A large dataset can remain unrepresentative even when its individual records are accurate.

These invented datasets force us to name a purpose. Dataset A may be suitable for checking one class’s activity, but it may not represent the whole school. Dataset B might capture a wider range of people, yet its errors require investigation. We cannot decide which is better without knowing the question, sampling, missingness and quality controls. Items with more exposure often receive more interactions. This could reflect relevance, the number of opportunities to be seen or both. Looking only at interaction totals cannot distinguish those explanations. A carefully designed comparison might hold exposure constant or compare a changed allocation, but even then we need to examine other factors and limits. Why does a lifecycle model matter?

Read the explanation and source notes

The chapter places big data inside a lifecycle. At collection, we decide what to record and whom to include. Storage involves access, security and organization. Use involves analysis and sharing for a purpose. Preservation and destruction concern how long records remain and what happens when they are no longer needed. This week’s assigned reading begins the collection dilemmas in section 3.1I. It does not teach all later lifecycle dilemmas. A diagram of a lifecycle is a map, not proof that every stage has been adequately managed.

The assigned page raises ethical collection, consent and excessive collection. We can ask what information is actually needed to answer a question, and whether a group is missing before analysis begins. Collecting an additional personal detail can create a privacy cost without improving the decision. Applicable rules depend on the setting and jurisdiction, so this is an ethical design discussion rather than a universal legal checklist. Explain the purpose, the proposed data and the effect of collecting or omitting it.

The textbook gives historical examples from finance and sports. The enduring idea is the use of data to inform a decision. Describing last month’s activity, flagging an unusual transaction and predicting interest are different tasks. A prediction expresses an estimated outcome and should be evaluated against later observations. The examples do not make analytics infallible. A false alert can inconvenience someone, while a missed alert can leave a problem undetected. Identify the task and the cost of each kind of error.
